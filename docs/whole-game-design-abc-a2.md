# Pirate Seas: complete learning journey, ABC → A1 → A2

Design proposal · revised 13 September 2026 · Detailed through A1; A2 is a roadmap.

This document expands and revises the existing curriculum, Island 3/4 specifications, and technical architecture. It remains a design specification, not a claim that the entire journey exists. The deployed browser prototype now includes Starting Harbor, five foundation units, and a reusable seven-mission Name Island. Later islands, production recordings/artwork, a true retention model, and the full assessment system still require implementation and content production.

## 1. Product decision and destination

Every new learner starts at **Starting Harbor (נמל ההתחלה)**, with no assumed English, alphabet knowledge, reading direction, or knowledge of game controls. Name Island becomes the first *thematic* island after the foundations. It is no longer the starting screen for a fresh profile.

The game develops two related skills in parallel: understanding/using English, and reading/writing English. Knowing the alphabet is useful but does not itself demonstrate English proficiency. Children begin hearing and using meaningful English on day one; they do not have to memorize 26 letter names before saying hello.

| Stage | World | Scope | Destination |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundations | The Launching Cove | 6 short units, 26 core mini-missions | Understand controls, hear English sounds, encounter every letter, start blending if ready |
| Pre-A1 | The Calm Sea | 12 thematic islands × 7 missions | Understand and use familiar words and short phrases in everyday scenes |
| A1 | The Trade Winds | 16 islands × 7 missions | Handle supported everyday exchanges; describe people, places, routines, and simple events |
| A2 | The Far Ocean | 12 proposed islands | Handle routine transactions, exchange information, and connect simple descriptions and stories |

Through A1 this is **34 units and 222 first-pass core missions**: 26 foundation missions + 84 Pre-A1 missions + 112 A1 missions. Reviews, literacy extensions, optional speaking, and return visits are additional. These numbers describe content scope, not the time needed to acquire a language.

Planning targets for a deduplicated vocabulary inventory are approximately 280–360 receptive word families by the Pre-A1 checkpoint, 600–800 by A1, and 1,000–1,400 by A2. These are proposed production budgets, not CEFR requirements, verified word counts, or promises of proficiency. Reuse across islands does not count as new vocabulary. Phrase frames have a separate inventory.

### External alignment

CEFR describes what a learner can do with language. At A1, supported understanding of familiar questions and short directions matters; the product must collect evidence of communication rather than infer a level from coins or completed screens. See the [Council of Europe descriptor reference](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/cefr-descriptors-search).

Cambridge's child-oriented sequence maps Pre-A1 Starters, A1 Movers, and A2 Flyers to useful content references. Its A1 Movers grammar list already includes simple past and comparisons. This proposal therefore introduces those before the A1 endpoint, correcting the older roadmap's decision to postpone them entirely to A2. This is a curriculum choice informed by Cambridge, not a claim that CEFR prescribes one grammar order. See the [Cambridge teacher handbook](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/357180-starters-movers-and-flyers-handbook-for-teachers-2024.pdf) and [young learner qualifications overview](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/qualifications-young-learners/).

The island order, story, games, sample language, thresholds, and production estimates below are original product proposals. They are not a reproduction of an exam syllabus or a complete Cambridge exam-preparation course.

## 2. The game children experience

### Story and motivation

The child is a new crew member helping a young parrot repair the **Map of Friendly Seas**. The map's landmarks respond to English. Letters light a lighthouse, descriptions paint a reef, questions locate a missing pet, and a clear request supplies a ship.

- Launching Cove: build the small boat and learn how its signs and sounds work.
- Calm Sea: meet the crew and furnish the home harbor through everyday language.
- Trade Winds: help neighboring islands trade, travel, organize events, and solve small mysteries.
- Far Ocean: plan a voyage, explain what happened, compare choices, and help other characters cooperate.

There is no combat, punishment for errors, lost life, public ranking, or compulsory countdown. A “boss” is a friendly character with a real communicative need. Finishing changes the scene: the lighthouse shines, the octopus regains color, the café opens, or a friend gets home.

### Main screens

1. Parent setup: child nickname/avatar, learner mode, audio check, optional placement. No email or identifying information is required to play locally.
2. Harbor: one large Continue button, a small Review dock, parrot companion, and optional home decoration.
3. World map: current destination prominent; explored islands can always be revisited. Locked content shows what help is needed, not a failing grade.
4. Island scene: seven mission landmarks and a visible story problem.
5. Activity: one task at a time, large targets, replay audio, demo replay, pause/back, gentle feedback.
6. Celebration: what the child did, cosmetic rewards, and a natural stopping point.
7. Parent zone: separate evidence for listening, speaking, reading, writing, and retained knowledge.

### Early-learner interaction rule: one idea, several senses

For Starting Harbor and the first foundation encounters, each round teaches **one concrete idea** through coordinated channels: the child sees it, touches or moves it, hears the same English meaning, and sees an immediate result that still represents that idea. Art, speech, prompt, choices, and outcome must all point to the same learning target. Decorative objects must not look like answers, and unrelated objects must not be used as distractors before the target concept is secure.

For example, a flag round shows a flag on a mast, offers different **flag designs** rather than unrelated symbols or objects, says “flag” when a design is touched, and places the chosen design on the same mast. The completed scene remains visibly a flag so the child can connect the English word, the image, the action, and the result.

Visible Hebrew is a fallback cue, not the primary explanation. F00 instructions have one action, preferably one short line, and a hard maximum of 30 visible characters. Animation and audio demonstrate the action. F01–F05 use the same one-action principle and must be tested with young children for reading load before content expands.

### Learner paths

| Feature | Pre-reader / listening path | Reader / literacy path |
|---|---|---|
| Navigation | Pictures, spoken Hebrew help, animated demonstrations | Same controls plus Hebrew labels |
| Target language | English audio and scenes; no answer requires reading | Audio, scenes, and controlled written English |
| ABC activities | Optional symbol exploration and sound play | Structured letter-sound, blending, and spelling sequence |
| Sentence building | Hearable picture/chunk tiles with actions | Hearable word/chunk tiles arranged LTR |
| Response selection | First tap previews audio; separate confirm commits choice | Same behavior, with visible English |
| Speaking | Optional mimicry or recorded response | Optional responses, then increasingly independent short speech |
| Reading/writing evidence | Not assessed until enabled | Collected separately from oral evidence |

Modes are based on readiness, not a hard age cutoff. Switching mode preserves oral progress and starts literacy at its own checkpoint. A pre-reader can progress orally without passing spelling tests; the parent dashboard must not report untested reading/writing as achieved A1.

### Typical session

Pre-readers: 5–8 minutes, normally one short new mission and a small review. Readers: 8–12 minutes, normally one or two missions plus review. A level pauses between rounds and resumes without losing earned work. The old “2–3 full levels plus review” default would exceed the intended time budget for many children.

Returning session: 1–2 minutes of due review → one new story mission → optional sound/reading practice → celebration and a choice to stop. First session has no review of unseen content. The Treasure Dive's 90 seconds is an approximate content budget, never a visible deadline.

## 3. Foundations: a true zero-English start

Teach letter **sounds**, letter **names**, and letter **shapes** as distinct things. Show lowercase first for reading; pair uppercase after the lowercase form is recognizable. The alphabet song is optional enjoyment, not a progress test. Alphabetical order and dictionary-style lookup come later.

The proposed sound order is selected to allow useful short words early. A literacy specialist must review the exact recordings, word eligibility, and accent assumptions before production. Example words below must first be introduced orally and pictured. They cannot enter a decoding exercise solely because their letters have been taught.

### F00 — Starting Harbor (נמל ההתחלה): “Meet your parrot” · 4 missions

**Outcome:** tap, listen, replay, choose, and stop without reading an instruction. **English:** hello, bye, yes, no; listen, look, go, stop as modeled control language.

1. Parrot waves; tap the waving hand; hear “Hello!” Optional cheerful repetition.
2. Audio check with an obvious picture-action pair; replay button demonstrated.
3. Follow modeled go/stop commands to move a boat. No timing test.
4. See a large blank flag on a mast. Touch one of four clearly recognizable flag designs to hear “flag” plus its design, place that same design on the mast, hear “This is my flag,” and say goodbye to the harbor guide. No unrelated object is presented as a flag choice.

First-choice failure triggers a hand animation and a new attempt. Completion evidence is successful interaction with help available; no alphabet knowledge is required. Parent may enter a nickname, but the child may use a fictional captain name. Every F00 Hebrew instruction must pass the 30-character content check, and a child must be able to act from the modeled scene without listening to a long Hebrew explanation.

### F01 — Lantern Letters · 4 missions

**Reader targets:** s, a, t, p, i, n; recognize initial sounds and follow a glowing LTR trail. **Word pool:** sat, sit, pin, pan, tap, tin, introduced as pictures/actions in small batches.

1. Light s/a/t lanterns with sound-picture introductions.
2. Add p/i/n; choose a starting sound from two or three options.
3. Connect sound to lowercase symbol; then explore its uppercase partner.
4. Move three sound lights together to blend one or two taught words; optional spoken response.

Pre-reader version uses first-sound sorting and oral blending with pictures. It never asks the child to distinguish printed s from t. **Gate:** demonstrate control of the current sound set with assistance available; weaker correspondences recur before more literacy load is added.

### F02 — Bottle Beach · 4 missions

**Targets:** m, d, g, o, c, k; c and k share the modeled /k/ sound in eligible words. **Words:** map, mat, dog, dot, dig, cat, cap, cot.

1. Meet three new sounds and their objects; 2. introduce the other three in another short encounter; 3. sort beginning/final sounds and match cases; 4. blend and build map/cat/dog from sounds.

The bottle machine receives phonemes in LTR order and reveals the pictured word. It does not say letter names during blending. Two symbols representing a similar sound are explicitly modeled, not treated as an error in the child’s listening.

### F03 — Short-Vowel Lagoon · 4 missions

**Targets:** e, u, r, h, b, f, l. **Words:** pen, hen, red, bed, cup, sun, run, hat, hot, bat, fat, lap. Deliver this inventory across returns, never as thirteen new words in one lesson.

1. e/u sound-picture contrasts; 2. r/h/b; 3. f/l and case pairs; 4. change one sound in a taught CVC word and observe its picture change.

Contrast hat/hot or pen/pin only after both meanings are familiar. Pre-readers choose spoken minimal contrasts from pictures. **Evidence:** distinguish familiar words without copying the narrator's highlighted answer.

### F04 — Alphabet Lookout · 5 missions

**Targets:** j, v, w, x, y, z, q; model qu together in words. **Words:** jam, van, wet, wax, yes, zip, quiz. All are pictured/acted first; quiz is enrichment rather than a compulsory early noun.

1. j/v/w; 2. x/y/z; 3. q with u and /kw/; 4. mixed uppercase/lowercase matching across the alphabet; 5. find letters out of alphabetical order to repair the lookout sign.

Teach /w/ with lip movement, contrasted gently with /v/. Show that x commonly represents two sounds in box/wax; do not force “one letter = one sound.” The completed alphabet chart is a tap-to-hear collection, with distinct letter-name and sound buttons for Readers.

### F05 — First-Word Lighthouse · 5 missions

**Targets:** consolidate familiar short vowels, LTR blending and segmenting, and spaces between words. **Words:** only vocabulary already introduced in F01–F04. Introduce a few useful irregular high-frequency forms explicitly, such as I and a, with a separate learning tag.

1. Read a new arrangement of taught sounds; 2. hear a word and build it; 3. replace its first or final sound; 4. match a short controlled caption to a scene; 5. light the lighthouse using three independent sound/word tasks.

Reader example: “a cat” or “a red hat” after all components are known. Pre-reader example: hear “a red hat” and select the depicted combination, with red introduced orally before the task.

**End-of-foundations evidence:** Readers can identify taught correspondences in random order and blend a small fresh sample of eligible CVC words. Pre-readers can use the app, discriminate familiar sounds, and respond to basic spoken language. Neither outcome requires alphabet recital or compulsory recording.

### First 10 minutes, concretely

Minute 0–2: choose avatar with parent; audio check; meet parrot. Minute 2–4: hello/go/stop through action. Minute 4–6: explore s/a/t with pictures and sounds. Minute 6–8: retrieve two familiar choices and play with one sound trail. Minute 8–10 is optional: decorate the boat and stop. The next session resumes at the next small encounter, not the beginning of a four-mission unit.

## 4. The seven-mission island template

An island is a thematic chapter that takes several sessions, not a single long lesson. Each mission contains short rounds. Its content budget is typically 18–25 new words and 3–5 phrase frames, distributed over the whole island. New recognition rounds normally introduce 3–5 items; later scenes may expose a few more without grading them yet.

| Mission | Learning purpose | Reusable mechanic |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Discover | Hear meaning before testing | Explore a scene; narrator models words and actions |
| 2 Retrieve | Remember sound–meaning links | Audio/picture matches; position and artwork vary |
| 3 Sound / Read | Build phonological or literacy skill | Picture sound games / phonics, captions, short writing |
| 4 Act | Understand language in context | Execute spoken commands or solve a scene task |
| 5 Build | Express a relation, not only name an object | Sentence bridge, request builder, timeline, description |
| 6 Revisit | Retain and transfer earlier learning | Compatible due items from prior islands, plus this island when needed |
| 7 Meet | Use language for a character's goal | Scripted dialogue, picture description, information gap, or role-play |

Each unit below specifies all seven missions in this order. Its listed vocabulary is the **mandatory core/sample inventory**, not a finalized complete word list. Before authoring, expand into a deduplicated manifest that includes every noun, verb, instruction, dialogue line, distractor, and shop label. Mark language as taught, newly modeled, or optional narrative decoration. Never assess an untaught item.

The first challenge options must always include a correct answer. Hearing an answer card to preview it must not submit it. After a modeled correction, the next scored attempt uses a fresh arrangement and does not count as independent simply because the child immediately copies it.

## 5. Calm Sea: Pre-A1 in detail

### P01 — Name Island: meet the crew

**Can do:** greet, choose/give a name, and answer a simple feeling question. **Core:** hello, hi, bye, goodbye, boy, girl, friend, teacher, man, woman, name, happy, sad, fine, yes, no, please, thank you. **Frames:** “My name is …”; “I'm …”; “How are you?”; “This is my friend.”

**Missions:** 1 meet animated islanders; 2 audio-person pairs; 3 first sounds / eligible word building plus explicitly taught irregular forms; 4 wave/walk/say hello commands, all verbs modeled first; 5 copula plank in “I am happy”; 6 familiar harbor words in new artwork; 7 Captain Morgan greets, asks name, asks feeling, introduces the parrot, and says goodbye.

**Dialogue variation:** happy/sad/fine all accepted; sad gets empathy, not pressure to become happy. Name slot accepts a chosen nickname without runtime name TTS. **Evidence:** independent appropriate greeting and feeling response; separate evidence for including the copula. Do not label girl, boy, or name as simple short-vowel CVC words.

### P02 — Rainbow Reef: colors and quantities 1–5

**Can do:** identify color, count a small set, and describe a colored object. **Core:** red, blue, yellow, green, orange, pink, black, white, one–five, shell, star, color, look. **Frames:** “It's red”; “a red shell”; “How many?”; “Two shells.”

**Missions:** 1 paint reef features by hearing colors; 2 audio-to-swatch and audio-to-quantity pairs; 3 color first sounds / red plus individually introduced camera words; 4 select two red shells; 5 paint-bucket adjective order and plural sticker; 6 greetings plus color/object retrieval; 7 restore octopus arms by describing colors and counting shells.

**Variation/evidence:** change objects as well as positions; any matching red object can answer an open color request. Measure adjective placement and plural inclusion separately. A counting scaffold is allowed but recorded as help. Splitting the old Rainbow Reef inventory reduces simultaneous color, number, and grammar load.

### P03 — Counting Cove: numbers 6–20 and shapes

**Can do:** count objects, distinguish numerals from quantities, and give an age. **Core:** six–twenty, circle, square, triangle, number, count; purple/brown/gray arrive in small color extensions. **Frames:** “I'm seven”; “There are ten” as a modeled chunk; “How old are you?”

**Missions:** 1 load numbered cargo from small sets to larger sets; 2 number-audio/quantity matching; 3 clap syllables / number-word reading with irregular spellings explicitly modeled; 4 deliver a requested number of flags; 5 build age and quantity messages; 6 colors with number quantities; 7 help the cargo keeper check three orders and make an optional fictional birthday badge.

**Evidence:** cardinality after counting, not just reciting a number chain. Distribute teen contrasts across sessions; counting to 20 is not required in the first visit.

### P04 — Toy Treasure: objects and location

**Can do:** name belongings and locate an object. **Core:** ball, doll, kite, teddy, robot, car, train, boat, box, bag, big, small, in, on, under, here, there. **Frames:** “It's a …”; “Where is the …?”; “It's under the box”; “my/your …”.

**Missions:** 1 open named toy chests; 2 match audio to toy/location scenes; 3 sound sorting / decodable toy labels; 4 put a ball in/on/under a box; 5 place hearable location tiles into a scene sentence; 6 colors and quantities; 7 find a lost toy by asking where it is and following a clue.

**Evidence:** act correctly on novel spatial layouts and ask/choose a useful location question. Do not teach the same object as always appearing in the same place.

### P05 — Animal Island: pets, articles, and abilities

**Can do:** identify common animals and describe a simple ability. **Core:** cat, dog, bird, fish, rabbit, duck, cow, horse, sheep, goat, tail, pet, run, jump, swim, fly, eat, sleep. **Frames:** “It's a/an …”; “A bird can fly”; “Can it swim?”; “Yes, it can.”

**Missions:** 1 animal care discovery; 2 animal-audio/picture retrieval; 3 animal sound contrast / controlled decoding; 4 feed or move the named animal; 5 article ticket plus can-action bridge; 6 count familiar animals and describe their locations; 7 choose a suitable animal helper for a rescue.

**Evidence:** article and ability choices in new combinations. Teach irregular plurals such as fish/sheep as marked exceptions; the plural-sticker mechanic must not incorrectly add -s to every animal.

### P06 — Family Bay: people and belonging

**Can do:** introduce people in a picture and say who owns something. **Core:** mother, father, sister, brother, baby, grandmother, grandfather, family, child, children, he, she, they, his, her. **Frames:** “This is my …”; “He/She is …”; “They are …”; “Whose bag is it?” with a modeled answer.

**Missions:** 1 meet varied fictional families; 2 person-description pairs; 3 pronoun listening / readable captions; 4 deliver objects to named people; 5 subject–copula bridges; 6 names, toys, and feelings; 7 introduce three members of a chosen story crew and return an item to its owner.

**Evidence:** referent tracking rather than identifying gender from stereotyped clothes. A child may use a fictional crew; do not require disclosure of their own family structure.

### P07 — Body & Movement Island

**Can do:** identify body parts and follow action instructions. **Core:** head, face, eye, ear, nose, mouth, hair, arm, hand, leg, foot/feet, tooth/teeth, touch, clap, turn, stand, sit. **Frames:** “I have two …”; “Touch your …”; “Can you …?”

**Missions:** 1 build a friendly puppet; 2 audio/body-part retrieval; 3 initial/final sound games / short captions; 4 touch, clap, stand, and sit through avatar actions; 5 “I have” chunk and quantity bridges; 6 numbers and actions; 7 assemble a funny fictional creature from a character's description.

**Evidence:** complete new one-step and then two-step commands. All movement has a seated/tap alternative; camera-based body tracking is unnecessary.

### P08 — Food Lagoon: likes and polite requests

**Can do:** express a preference and request an item. **Core:** apple, banana, orange, bread, rice, egg, cheese, milk, water, juice, cake, sandwich, hungry, thirsty, like, want. **Frames:** “I like …”; “I don't like …”; “Can I have …, please?”; “Here you are.”

**Missions:** 1 explore picnic food; 2 listen-to-food matching; 3 syllables / familiar menu words; 4 prepare a modeled picnic order; 5 request and preference builder; 6 colors, amounts, and articles; 7 exchange greeting → request → receive → thank the café keeper.

**Evidence:** choose food according to a newly heard request and build a personally chosen request. All preferences are accepted; avoid rewarding a particular diet or food choice.

### P09 — Home Harbor: rooms and furniture

**Can do:** describe a room and place objects. **Core:** house, home, room, kitchen, bedroom, bathroom, living room, door, window, bed, chair, table, lamp, sofa, floor, garden. **Frames:** “There is a …”; “There are two …”; “The lamp is on the table.”

**Missions:** 1 furnish rooms with audio; 2 room/item retrieval; 3 sound/short room labels; 4 place objects by spoken instructions; 5 singular/plural there-is bridges; 6 toys and spatial words; 7 describe a furnished cabin so the builder can reproduce it.

**Evidence:** choose is/are with countable objects and communicate locations. Home decoration starts as free story play; earned cosmetics expand visual choice, never access to lessons.

### P10 — Clothes & Weather Cove

**Can do:** identify clothing and describe what someone wears now. **Core:** hat, shirt, T-shirt, dress, skirt, trousers, shorts, shoes, socks, coat, wear, hot, cold, sun, rain, sunny, rainy. **Frames:** “I'm wearing …”; “It's cold”; “Put on your …”.

**Missions:** 1 dress avatars; 2 garment/color pairs; 3 sound patterns / controlled labels; 4 follow outfit instructions; 5 combine wearing + garment, and recycle colors; 6 body parts and belongings; 7 dress a character for a simple weather scene and describe the outfit.

**Evidence:** match both garment and attribute. Several sensible outfit choices may work; the challenge evaluates understood language, not fashion or stereotypes.

### P11 — School Rock: understanding instructions

**Can do:** identify classroom objects, follow instructions, and ask for help. **Core:** school, classroom, book, page, pencil, pen, ruler, rubber/eraser, desk, board, bag, open, close, read, write, draw. **Frames:** “Open your book”; “This is … / These are …”; “Can you help me?”; “Again, please.”

**Missions:** 1 explore the ship's classroom; 2 object/instruction pairs; 3 segment sounds / decode and write eligible labels; 4 follow a two-step classroom instruction; 5 build a request for an object or help; 6 counts, location, and colors; 7 help the new crew member prepare a school bag and ask for missing supplies.

**Evidence:** appropriate help/clarification request plus an instruction followed without an animated hint. Accept British/American alternatives while keeping one consistent narrated version.

### P12 — Playday Port: Pre-A1 checkpoint

**Can do:** combine familiar language across people, play, food, and places. **Core additions:** play, sing, dance, read, draw, morning, afternoon, evening, today; other vocabulary is review. **Frames:** “I play …”; “I like …”; “Let's …”; “Where is …?”

**Missions:** 1 set up a picnic/playday; 2 mixed retrieval with unfamiliar artwork; 3 sound review / readable clue cards; 4 follow two familiar commands; 5 assemble a short self-description; 6 due review including weak skills; 7 help the harbor festival through greeting, name, age, preference, quantity, location, and farewell exchanges.

**Evidence:** varied prompts and delayed review. The seven-turn festival is a supported Pre-A1 checkpoint, not the A1 finish. A “Can greet” shield cannot be earned only by matching a word to its own visible label.

## 6. Trade Winds: A1 in detail

This stage increases independence and combines known language. It does not simply add longer word lists. Most missions mix previously taught language with one new communication target. Introduce no more than one major new grammatical contrast in a round.

### A01 — Morning Island: routines

**Core:** wake up, get up, wash, brush, get dressed, breakfast, lunch, dinner, go, come, work, school, every day. **Frames:** “I get up …”; “She gets up …”; “Do you …?”; “Yes, I do / No, I don't.” **Can do:** describe a familiar routine.

**Missions:** 1 animate a daily routine; 2 match spoken routines to sequences; 3 rhythm/sound / short routine captions; 4 follow a routine schedule; 5 build present-simple statements and a question; 6 school/body/food review; 7 interview a captain about two routine actions and describe one's own or a fictional day.

**Evidence:** distinguish a statement from a question; third-person -s is scaffolded and reused in later islands rather than mastered in one encounter.

### A02 — Clock Tower: time and dates

**Core:** twenty–one hundred in families, o'clock, half past, day, week, weekend, Monday–Sunday, before, after. Months are an optional later extension here and core in A14. **Frames:** “It's seven o'clock”; “On Monday …”; “at eight”; “When …?”

**Missions:** 1 set harbor clocks; 2 audio/time matching; 3 stress in teen/ty numbers / schedule reading; 4 deliver a ticket for the requested day/time; 5 build day/action/time messages; 6 routines and numbers; 7 agree when to meet a boat and identify the correct departure.

**Evidence:** interpret time from audio and schedule independently of fast arithmetic. Add quarter past/to only after whole and half hours are secure.

### A03 — Action Lagoon: now versus usually

**Core:** carry, cook, clean, climb, catch, kick, ride, watch, talk, now, usually; run/swim/play are review. **Frames:** “He is running”; “He runs every day”; “What is she doing?”

**Missions:** 1 compare an animated current scene with a routine strip; 2 hear and select the correct time context; 3 sound patterns / sentence-caption matching; 4 direct characters through an action scene; 5 build be + -ing against a present-simple alternative; 6 clothes and routines; 7 report what the crew is doing and what one member usually does.

**Evidence:** understand the temporal contrast on new scenes. This explicitly addresses Hebrew's lack of the same present simple/progressive distinction.

### A04 — Hobby Bay: preferences and ability

**Core:** football, basketball, tennis, badminton, cycling, fishing, swimming, painting, music, guitar, piano, enjoy, love, good at. **Frames:** “I like swimming”; “Can you …?”; “What do you like doing?”; “He can't …”.

**Missions:** 1 explore activity clubs; 2 match interests and abilities; 3 sound/club notice reading; 4 assemble equipment from instructions; 5 build preferences and can/can't descriptions; 6 animal abilities and routine questions; 7 choose a club for two characters using their different interests.

**Evidence:** ask a relevant question and use its answer. The right club varies by the supplied character profile, not by a fixed favorite activity.

### A05 — Town Island: places and directions

**Core:** street, road, park, shop, market, school, library, hospital, station, bus, bike, car, left, right, straight, near, next to, opposite. **Frames:** “Where's the …?”; “Go straight”; “Turn left”; “It's next to …”.

**Missions:** 1 reveal a small town map; 2 place/audio retrieval; 3 sound patterns / signs; 4 follow two- then three-step routes; 5 construct directions using landmarks; 6 home/spatial language; 7 ask for a place and guide a lost visitor there.

**Evidence:** reach destinations on more than one map. Distinguish UI RTL from physical left/right instructions; maps and directions never reverse automatically with the Hebrew interface.

### A06 — Market Island: quantities and transactions

**Core:** buy, sell, price, money, coin, cost, some, any, bottle, cup, bag, more, much, many; food inventory expands in small sets. **Frames:** “How much is …?”; “I'd like …”; “Have you got …?”; “Two, please.”

**Missions:** 1 browse a hearable market; 2 retrieve quantities and prices; 3 listening / read price tags and short menus; 4 fulfill a shopping list; 5 build request–price–thanks exchanges; 6 food, numbers, and polite chunks; 7 buy two items, ask about an unavailable item, and choose an alternative.

**Evidence:** recover from “We haven't got …” with help. Payments use pretend story coins; literacy/numeracy support must not prevent communication practice.

### A07 — Feelings & Help Reef

**Core:** tired, excited, worried, angry, scared, thirsty, hungry, hurt, headache, stomach ache, cold, rest, help, better. **Frames:** “What's the matter?”; “I've got …”; “I'm tired because …”; “Can you help me?”

**Missions:** 1 understand fictional characters' needs; 2 feeling/cause matching; 3 intonation / short help notes; 4 bring a requested comfort item; 5 connect feeling and simple cause; 6 body parts, food, and home; 7 ask what happened, understand a simple need, offer help, and check how the character feels.

**Evidence:** understand/produce because in a familiar causal scene. Content concerns fictional everyday situations; it does not assess the child's health or emotions. “Should” is optional modeled language here and developed in A2.

### A08 — Description Dock: people and belongings

**Core:** tall, short, long, curly, straight, glasses, beard, young, old, same, different; possessives and clothing are review. **Frames:** “She's got long hair”; “He's wearing …”; “Whose …?”; “It's hers”; “Give it to him.”

**Missions:** 1 meet a varied crew; 2 match two-feature descriptions; 3 listening / short character cards; 4 deliver lost belongings; 5 combine is/has/wearing descriptions; 6 family/clothes/body vocabulary; 7 identify a missing passenger from an unseen character's description.

**Evidence:** use two meaningful clues, not a single obvious icon. Depict a range of appearances respectfully; avoid value judgments about bodies.

### A09 — Compare Cliffs

**Core:** bigger, smaller, taller, shorter, longer, faster, slower, stronger, better, best, than, most; underlying adjectives are introduced first if missing. **Frames:** “This boat is bigger than that boat”; “Which is the smallest?”

**Missions:** 1 compare real scene properties; 2 match spoken comparisons; 3 pronunciation / comparison captions; 4 choose equipment to fit a task; 5 comparison bridges with than; 6 animal/body/toy descriptions; 7 select a boat for a character using two requirements.

**Evidence:** infer a relation between objects; do not turn adjective recognition into arithmetic. Include a limited set of regular and irregular comparisons before expanding in A2.

### A10 — Yesterday Harbor: was and were

**Core:** yesterday, last night, last week, ago, was, were, wasn't, weren't, then; locations and feelings are review. **Frames:** “I was at home”; “They were happy”; “Where were you?”; “Was it …?”

**Missions:** 1 compare today/yesterday snapshots; 2 select past locations/feelings; 3 listening / short diary captions; 4 reconstruct where characters were; 5 was/were bridges; 6 present be and places; 7 interview two characters to locate a misplaced parcel.

**Evidence:** track time and subject using a new scene; an incorrect tense produces a model and later retry, not a failed adventure.

### A11 — Yesterday Workshop: regular past

**Core:** played, walked, visited, helped, cleaned, opened, closed, watched, wanted, needed; base verbs are modeled or reviewed first. **Frames:** “Yesterday I played …”; “Did you …?”; “I didn't …”.

**Missions:** 1 animate completed actions; 2 action/time matching; 3 hear -ed endings / read familiar past forms; 4 sequence yesterday's jobs; 5 build positive/negative/question forms; 6 routines and yesterday locations; 7 explain which jobs a character completed and ask about one missing task.

**Evidence:** after did/didn't, the verb returns to its base form. -ed pronunciation receives exposure and mimicry; exact phonetic performance never gates progress.

### A12 — Adventure Postcards: common irregular past

**Core pairs:** go/went, see/saw, eat/ate, drink/drank, have/had, get/got, make/made, take/took, buy/bought, find/found. Introduce three or four pairs per session, not all ten at once. **Frames:** “We went to …”; “What did you see?”; “I saw …”.

**Missions:** 1 discover a three-picture trip; 2 hear past events and select scenes; 3 sound patterns / postcard captions; 4 reconstruct event order; 5 combine time, person, action, and object; 6 market/town/food language; 7 exchange a short postcard story with a character and answer two questions.

**Evidence:** retrieve useful irregular forms in familiar contexts. Recognition of a base/past pair alone does not earn a storytelling shield.

### A13 — Clue Cave: questions and short stories

**Core:** first, next, then, last, because, but, why, when, what happened, find, lose, remember. **Frames:** “Why did …?”; “Because …”; “First … Then …”.

**Missions:** 1 explore a harmless missing-object mystery; 2 match clues to events; 3 question intonation / read three short clues; 4 ask one useful question at each scene; 5 arrange and retell a three-event story; 6 locations and past actions; 7 explain where an object went using evidence gathered through dialogue.

**Evidence:** choose relevant questions and convey sequence/cause. The child is allowed to request repetition; the solution and artwork change on replay.

### A14 — Calendar Festival: invitations and simple plans

**Core:** months, birthday, party, invite, tomorrow, next week, bring, meet, start, finish, together. **Frames:** “Would you like to …?”; “Yes, please / Sorry, I can't”; “Let's …”; “I'm going to …” as a supported plan frame.

**Missions:** 1 set festival dates; 2 match invitations and responses; 3 listening / short invitation cards; 4 prepare for an event from instructions; 5 build invitation, plan, and polite refusal; 6 time, activities, and food; 7 agree an activity and meeting time, adapting when a character is unavailable.

**Evidence:** complete a short invitation exchange with a meaningful choice. Detailed tense comparisons and broader future expression belong in A2.

### A15 — Journey Jetty: trips, requests, and rules

**Core:** ticket, trip, bus, train, plane, boat, arrive, leave, wait, carry, need, must, mustn't, have to, slowly, carefully. **Frames:** “I need a ticket”; “We must wait”; “Can you show me …?”; “I want to go …”.

**Missions:** 1 explore a transport hub; 2 match tickets/instructions to destinations; 3 listening / signs and simple timetables; 4 pack and board following instructions; 5 request/obligation builders; 6 directions, time, prices, and plans; 7 ask for travel help and resolve a changed departure.

**Evidence:** convey destination, understand one simple rule, and request clarification. Complex travel bureaucracy and emergencies are excluded from children's scenarios.

### A16 — Captain's World Fair: A1 checkpoint

**New vocabulary:** minimal; only necessary story labels are modeled. **Can do:** integrate descriptions, routine questions, requests, directions, and a short account of a familiar event.

**Missions:** 1 prepare a fair using spoken instructions; 2 identify people and objects from new descriptions; 3 readers interpret short signs/messages and produce a short note; pre-readers receive a separate oral task; 4 navigate and complete a small purchase; 5 tell a three-picture story and ask a question; 6 delayed mixed review; 7 an 8–10 turn captain conversation with genuine choices and a clarification opportunity.

**Sample boss arc:** greeting → name/preference → describe a visitor → ask where a stall is → request an item → understand its quantity/price → describe one event from yesterday → ask the captain a question → clarify a misunderstood detail → goodbye. Split across short encounters if needed.

**Evidence:** aggregate results across several contexts and sessions. Tapping modeled responses demonstrates supported selection, not independently verified spoken A1. Invite a short parent/teacher observation or optional recorded response to substantiate production claims.

## 7. Literacy progression woven through the islands

All reading exercises declare prerequisites at the grapheme–phoneme level, not simply “letters known.” The content engine chooses reading items that the learner can decode; otherwise it offers sound play or explicitly introduces the irregular spelling. Typography and unfamiliar pictures must not quietly raise difficulty.

| Window | Literacy focus | Example practice |
|---|---|---|
| F01–F05 | Lowercase sound links, case pairing, short vowels, LTR blending | pin, cat, dog, sun; only after oral introduction |
| P01–P03 | Consolidate CVC; introduce sh/ch/th/ng in small modules | ship, chat, thin, ring; both th sounds modeled separately |
| P04–P06 | Final then initial consonant clusters | hand, nest, frog; every constituent correspondence already introduced |
| P07–P09 | Common split digraphs, paced one pattern at a time | name, bike, home; distinguish silent final e from another syllable |
| P10–P12 | Frequent vowel teams and explicit exceptions | rain, play, green, boat; blue/one/two are not mislabeled decodable |
| A01–A04 | More vowel patterns, common endings, sentence fluency | -s, -ing; compare short controlled captions |
| A05–A08 | ar/or and accent-reviewed er/ir/ur; longer words | sound-to-spelling mapping with a consistent recorded accent |
| A09–A12 | -er/-est/-ed, irregular past forms, punctuation | read a short description or postcard; hear the model afterwards |
| A13–A16 | Independent short reading/writing with support options | understand a sign, extract details, write a short purposeful note |

Examples here are proposed pattern illustrations, not an approved decodability list. Multiple pronunciations and accent-sensitive spellings need explicit metadata. A word is not universally “decodable”; it is decodable under the learner's taught correspondences and the selected pronunciation.

Reader output grows from selecting a sound → arranging letters → labeling a picture → completing a phrase → writing a simple sentence → a short practical note. Keyboard navigation and touch entry both work. Handwriting/tracing is optional and never a motor-precision gate.

## 8. A2 roadmap: broader language and more independence

Keep the same world and activity engines, but extend dialogue, information gaps, text length, and freedom of expression. These are proposed chapters; full scripts and inventories follow validation of the A1 path.

| Island | Communicative purpose | Language development | Signature quest |
|---|---|---|---|
| B01 Tomorrow Isles | Explain plans and make offers | Extend going to; introduce useful will offers/predictions | Plan a two-day expedition |
| B02 Storm Stories | Describe ongoing and interrupted past events | Past continuous with past simple; when/while | Reconstruct a storm from witnesses |
| B03 Explorer's Journal | Tell a connected experience | Expand irregular past, sequence, time expressions | Narrate a four-picture adventure |
| B04 Ever-Ever Bay | Discuss familiar experiences | Present perfect in tightly supported experience frames | Compare crew experience before a trip |
| B05 Choice Cliffs | Compare options and explain preference | Broader comparisons, superlatives, too/enough | Choose equipment under several constraints |
| B06 Home & Town Exchange | Exchange missing information | Expanded there-is, relative descriptions, directions | Rebuild a partner's town from clues |
| B07 Care Harbor | Ask for help, make suggestions | should/shouldn't; useful problem–solution language | Help a fictional tired crew prepare a rest day |
| B08 Market & Café II | Sustain a routine transaction | Quantity, price, alternatives, clarification | Complete an order when two items are unavailable |
| B09 Work & Nature Isles | Describe roles, facts, and reasons | Present simple, frequency, want to, connectors | Match helpers to an island restoration job |
| B10 Invitation Cape | Arrange and negotiate an activity | Offers, polite requests/refusals, agreeing alternatives | Organize a party around different preferences |
| B11 Message Island | Understand and produce useful short texts | Invitations, directions, messages, connected sentences | Solve a quest through a notice, message, and reply |
| B12 Pirate Council | Combine language across everyday situations | Transfer and repair; no major new grammar | Information gap + story + transaction + conversation |

A2 readers work with gradually longer messages and simple connected texts, initially around 40–80 words and later 80–150 where appropriate. Proposed writing tasks move toward a purposeful 4–6 sentence note or description. These are adjustable content-length guidelines, not CEFR scoring requirements. Younger oral-path learners work with scene sequences and conversation instead.

The final council asks the learner to identify differences between scenes, obtain missing information, narrate a short sequence, agree a plan, and repair a misunderstanding. The game can demonstrate A2-related abilities in these scenarios; it cannot award external certification. Full A2 claims require sufficient independent evidence across skills.

No open AI conversation is needed for this roadmap. Scripted characters with multiple valid responses and controlled variables can provide meaningful choice through A2. AI is a separate future product decision, not a prerequisite.

## 9. Progression, retention, and assessment

### Separate four concepts

- **Story completion:** the mission was finished, including with help.
- **Skill evidence:** what was understood or produced, with mode and assistance recorded.
- **Retention:** whether the item can be retrieved again after a delay.
- **Rewards:** recognition of participation and functional milestones.

Never compute “mastery” from completed levels. The deployed prototype currently does this and should label its meter “journey progress” until an actual retention model exists.

### Proposed memory model

Track each word/phrase with separate receptive, productive, and literacy evidence where relevant. “New” means no assessed encounter; “Learning” means introduced; “Known” means repeated independent retrieval including delayed evidence; “Stable” means retrieval across longer delays. Overdue items become due for review, not forgotten by decree.

Initial review intervals: end of session for a struggled item, then approximately 1, 3, 7, 14, and 30 days. These are tunable starting values. Do not advance several memory stages through rapid repeated taps or a replay in the same minute.

Record item ID, skill, activity, correctness, assistance, prompt variant, response type, first attempt, timestamp, and content version. “Assisted” includes a revealed answer, corrected demonstration, or semantic hint; a normal replay can remain unassisted listening evidence if the target task explicitly allows repetition. Record replay count separately.

### Unlocks that do not trap a beginner

Completing the boss opens the next story destination when essential prerequisite concepts have been encountered. If a critical prerequisite is missing, offer a short guided preparation mission and then allow exploration. Delayed retention affects the recommended review route and retained-skill badges; it does not strand a child behind an arbitrary multi-day wait.

This deliberately revises the older global “boss + 80% Known” hard lock. Keep an 80% target as a configurable *review signal*, not an unquestioned gate. Never require a nonexistent island or count unavailable pre-reader spelling tasks in a prerequisite denominator.

### Evidence for a can-do shield

Proposed initial rule: meaningful success in two different tasks, with at least one independent response and a later review opportunity. Thresholds must be calibrated with playtests. A “Practised” badge may appear immediately; “Remembered” appears after delayed evidence. Previously earned achievements are not revoked when an item becomes due.

Measure communication and target form separately. A bare color can successfully answer a color question; the character models the full phrase, while sentence-form evidence remains pending. A microphone skip is not incorrect. Automatic recognition cannot be the only evidence of success or failure.

### Assessment checkpoints

| Checkpoint | Tasks | Evidence to report |
|---|---|---|
| Foundations | Sound discrimination; Reader symbol matching/blending; basic commands | Listening readiness and literacy readiness, separately |
| P12 | Greeting, person/object identification, quantity, location, preference | Supported Pre-A1 abilities plus retained items |
| A08 midpoint | Description, routine, help request, directions | Skills secure / developing / not yet observed |
| A16 | Novel scene, transaction, brief narrative, question and clarification; Reader text tasks | A1-related skill profile with support level visible |
| B12 | Information gap, connected story, plan, routine exchange, text tasks | A2-related skill profile with production evidence |

Placement is parent-accessible and optional: a 5–8 minute branching voyage samples receptive vocabulary, commands, simple dialogue, and literacy separately. Placement can skip mastered content without pretending that reading ability implies oral fluency. Recheck skipped prerequisites inside early missions.

## 10. Difficulty, feedback, and transfer

After repeated errors, reduce one difficulty dimension at a time: fewer distractors → clearer picture distinctions → slower model → demonstration. Then retry later with a fresh prompt. Do not simultaneously add vocabulary, a new tense, an unfamiliar mechanic, and more answer choices. For foundation learners, do not manufacture difficulty with semantically unrelated distractors: vary examples *within* the concept after the concept is recognizable.

For missing copula: show the bridge gap, model the complete phrase, invite a repeat, and supply a fresh sentence. For adjective order: paint pours only onto the noun ahead of the adjective. For plurals: quantity and the noun ending agree visually, with exception rules. For did + verb: the question helper carries past marking so the main verb uses its base form.

Every skill needs transfer variants: different object, layout, character voice, response position, and task context. Distractors must be linguistically informative and all introduced. Use at least six prompt variants for a core target where feasible; variation in button position alone does not establish transfer.

## 11. Economy and the home island

Award a predictable base amount for a completed practice mission, with a small optional first-try bonus. Replays can earn a capped practice reward so returning learners can still decorate. Never award the full first-completion amount twice because a save was retried.

Pearls come from a first-time functional milestone, not every boss replay. The shop recycles language already taught: early picture-and-please exchanges grow into “Can I have …?”, quantities, prices, comparisons, and polite alternatives. Shop items must have a learnable spoken label before the shop asks the child to identify them.

Home items provide free retrieval: tap a lamp to hear lamp; arrange toys to practice under/on; a visitor asks where something is. Cosmetic purchases do not affect progression. No child-facing spending prompts, loot boxes, scarcity countdowns, or reward withdrawal for missing days.

## 12. Content and implementation requirements

### Stable identities and migration

F/P/A/B identifiers in this plan express sequence, not a demand to rename stored content. Preserve existing `island-003` and `island-004` identities; add a world manifest with explicit order and prerequisites. If Rainbow Reef becomes two units, map existing progress deliberately and do not copy earned rewards into both units.

On first launch with a new profile, resolve the world manifest to Starting Harbor. Existing prototype players should see their Name Island progress and a recommendation to explore the foundations; do not silently delete progress. The Continue button resumes an incomplete mission or the next accessible one.

### Content contracts

Each island pack needs: versioned identity; prerequisites; can-do objectives; vocabulary/chunk IDs; oral introductions; scene objects; level rounds; permissible answers; distractors; mode overrides; phonics prerequisites; recording references; dialogue graph; review compatibility; shield evidence; rewards; asset manifest; and, for each early-learner round, a declared focal concept with its visual, audio, action, and outcome representations.

Every assessed word/phrase maps to its teaching event. Every response records whether it was recognition, scaffolded construction, or independent production. A content linter rejects missing assets, empty answer sets, absent correct choices, unresolved variables, unreachable dialogue ends, impossible shields, prerequisite cycles, spelling tasks above the declared phonics level, F00 instructions over 30 visible characters, and early answer sets that mix the focal concept with unrelated object categories.

Reusable engines stay independent from React. The long-term target remains Expo/React Native with a web export, offline content, transactional persistence, and an activity registry. The current single-file browser implementation is a prototype and needs extraction into these engines before dozens of islands are authored.

### Reusable activity families to build

1. Scene exploration and audio introductions.
2. Audio/picture/category/quantity retrieval.
3. Sound sorting, oral blending, syllables, phonics, and word construction.
4. Action scene: movement, counting, placing, dressing, shopping, and routing.
5. Sentence builder with planks, articles, adjective order, plural endings, and tense helpers.
6. Story sequencing, timeline, and picture description.
7. Scripted dialogue and information-gap interpreter with multiple valid answers.
8. Short reading and writing tasks for Readers, with separately scored assistance.

Island-specific behavior belongs in content configuration where possible. Prove this with two different islands per engine before declaring it reusable. “JSON only” is a content-factory milestone after the necessary mechanics have actually been implemented.

### Audio and visual production

Learning audio must be recorded by a consistent native-speaker cast before comprehension playtesting. Record normal and supportive versions, phonemes, full chunks, commands, feedback, and dialogue variants. Do not synthesize phonemes by slicing arbitrary word recordings. Track filename, script, speaker, pronunciation, speed, rights, and review status in a recording manifest.

Art needs recognizable characters, actions, quantities, spatial relations, and contrast pairs. Never rely on emoji alone for ambiguous meanings such as goodbye/friend or on color alone to distinguish controls. Response pictures for abstract chunks need a hearable preview and a simple demonstrated scene.

### Quality and accessibility

Use large touch targets, predictable controls, keyboard support, pause/resume, independent audio volume, replay, reduced-motion support, and clear non-color selection indicators. Test Hebrew chrome and LTR learning content separately. Sentence order must remain English LTR on both web and Android.

Persist after each meaningful checkpoint. Completion, memory updates, and rewards should commit together with a stable attempt ID, making repeated save calls safe. Provide an understandable recovery path when browser storage is unavailable. Offline tests must include recorded audio and resumed sessions, not only the home screen.

## 13. Build order and acceptance gates

| Milestone | Deliverable | Exit criterion |
|---|---|---|
| M0 Correct entry and foundations | Profile flow, world manifest, Starting Harbor, F01–F05 | A fresh child can start without reading and reach a meaningful first success; F00 passes the short-instruction and one-concept/many-senses checks |
| M1 Reliable reusable slice | Refactored Name Island, actual audio, SRS, evidence, transactional rewards | All missions completable in both modes; saved progress survives restart; no false mastery claims |
| M2 Pre-A1 grammar/template proof | Split Rainbow/Counting, Toy, Animal islands | Paint, plural, quantity, placement, and exception handling work from content |
| M3 Complete Calm Sea | P01–P12, literacy thread, P12 checkpoint | Full Pre-A1 path, content lint, controlled reader vocabulary, delayed review |
| M4 Core A1 | A01–A08 | Routine, time, current actions, requests, directions, and descriptions transfer to new scenes |
| M5 Complete A1 | A09–A16, A1 profile, parent observations | Comparisons, past, short narrative, practical reading/writing, and communication assessed separately |
| M6 A2 production | Full inventories/scripts for B01–B12 | Proceed after A1 retention and production data support the design |

For each major mechanic, observe at least five children in each relevant learner mode as an initial usability check, including low-spec tablet sessions. This is a small design pilot, not an efficacy study. Include repeat visits to test delayed retrieval; a single sitting cannot validate spaced learning.

Track whether children understand the mechanic, require adult rescue, can find replay/help, encounter ambiguous pictures, and show fatigue. Review learning targets at a later session and around day seven. Report what was actually observed; do not invent retention thresholds or efficacy claims from a small pilot.

Priority before mass content authoring: a qualified early-literacy/English teacher reviews the phonics sequence, core vocabulary manifests, example sentences, and milestone rubric. This is production quality assurance, not a reason to delay implementing the basic content and activity infrastructure.

## 14. Decisions replacing the older draft

1. Beginner entry is Harbor + foundations, not Name Island.
2. ABC is meaningful sound–symbol learning alongside spoken English, not an alphabet-recitation gate.
3. Oral and literacy progression are independent, with separate evidence.
4. Rainbow Reef's color/number load is split across two chapters.
5. Short past accounts and comparisons enter before the A1 endpoint.
6. Story completion, retained knowledge, and independently demonstrated ability are different measurements.
7. Hard global retention locks become review recommendations plus targeted prerequisite help.
8. Camera words and grapheme prerequisites replace unsupported claims that every short word is decodable.
9. The seven-mission pattern includes variable sub-rounds; children are not expected to complete a whole island in one session.
10. A2 remains a scripted, assessable progression; neither an AI companion nor a microphone is required to play.

## 15. Authoring checklist for the next island

- Specify one communicative purpose and a final task that needs it.
- For each beginner round, name one focal concept and align its picture, touch action, audio, choices, and visible outcome; remove unrelated answer-like decoration.
- Declare all new words, chunks, grammar contrasts, sound patterns, and prerequisite concepts.
- Author the seven missions and both modes, with short rounds and demonstrated one-action instructions; enforce the F00 30-character maximum and test later foundation copy for equivalent reading load.
- Script every scored prompt, acceptable answer, correction, and alternate boss route.
- Ensure the answer appears among choices and the child can hear options before confirming.
- Include at least two transfer contexts and delayed-review items.
- Map every shield to evidence the available activities can actually emit.
- Produce and review recordings and pictures; label any temporary assets.
- Verify accessibility, RTL/LTR, save recovery, replay rewards, and offline playback.
- Playtest the distinctive mechanic, revise it, and only then expand the content inventory.
